Two-dimensional sketch operations

How Extend, Offset, Trim and Cut behave, drawn to scale. Each pane shows the state before the click on the left and the result on the right; the pointer position that produced the result is marked. This is a specification to agree on, not a screenshot of the editor.

Extend grow to meet

Extend lengthens an open end of a curve along its own direction until it runs into another curve. You point at the end you want to grow — not at the target — and the tool finds the first thing that end would hit if it kept going. Nothing else about the curve moves: the far end, and the curve's direction, are untouched. If the end would hit nothing, the tool refuses rather than guessing a length.

① The ordinary case — extend to a wall

The line keeps its direction and its far end. Only the clicked end travels, and it stops the instant it reaches the target.

② Which end you click decides which end grows

The same curve, the same two walls. Extend is directional: it always grows the end nearest your click.

The two decisions I need from you. ① When the extended end would cross several curves, I stop at the first one — the nearest. Plasticity does the same. ② When it would hit nothing, I refuse and leave the sketch alone, rather than extending by some arbitrary default length. Say if you would rather it extended to the sketch's outer bounds instead.

Offset parallel copy

Offset makes a new curve running parallel to the one you point at, at a fixed distance from it — the original is never modified. You drag to set the distance and the side, exactly as with the fillet; the popup shows the figure and takes an exact one back. Crossing the original with the pointer flips the offset to the other side, so one gesture covers both.

③ An open path — every segment moves out together

The corners of the copy sit where the two offset legs intersect — not where the original corner was pushed sideways. That is what keeps the distance constant round a bend.

④ A closed loop — inward shrinks, outward grows

Drag outward and the loop grows; drag through it and the same gesture produces the inward copy. An inward offset larger than the shape can take collapses it — so the drag is clamped, exactly as the fillet is.

The awkward case, stated rather than hidden. Offsetting a loop inward far enough makes short edges vanish and the copy self-intersect. Serious CAD removes those edges and re-stitches the result. My proposal: clamp the drag at the distance where the first edge would collapse, so the artist sees it stop. Full self-intersection cleanup is a much larger piece of work and I would rather do it deliberately than half-do it here.

Trim and Cut the pair that is easy to confuse

These two are opposites and they are worth putting side by side. Cut adds a division and removes nothing. Trim removes a piece between two existing divisions. Cut first, then trim what the cut created — that is the ordinary workflow.

⑤ Cut — a point divides, nothing is lost

Two cut points turn one circle into two arcs. Together they still close, so the transparent fill stays. Nothing was deleted — only divided.

⑥ Trim — a piece between two divisions is removed

The clicked arc is deleted. What remains is an open C, so the fill withdraws — the same rule the G Y lift already follows: a face needs a closed loop.

⑦ Your rule for the fill, drawn out

This is the rule as you stated it, and it is exactly what the engine already does. Filling asks one question — do the edges present close a loop? — and never asks how many edges there are or whether somebody cut them. Cutting an O into a C plus its missing piece leaves two edges that still close, so the fill stays. Delete the missing piece and they no longer close, so it goes. No special case is needed for cut, because cut removes nothing.

Other 2D operations worth having excluding booleans

You asked what else belongs in this family. These are the ones a 2D sketcher is normally expected to have. The right-hand column is my honest read of where Slate stands today.

OperationWhat it doesToday
Mirror Reflects selected curves across a line or axis, optionally keeping the original. The single most-used sketch operation after trim. tile exists, no geometry
Linear array Repeats a selection along a direction, n times at a spacing. Wants the same drag-plus-popup gesture as the fillet. tile exists, no geometry
Circular array Repeats a selection about a centre through a total angle. Distinct enough from the linear case to be its own tool. absent
Split at intersection Divides every curve at every crossing in one action. Cut does one point at a time; this is the bulk form, and it is what makes trim pleasant to use. absent
Join / merge The inverse of cut: two collinear or tangent edges sharing an end become one. Sketches accumulate needless divisions without it. absent
Close / bridge Draws the shortest segment between two open ends, so a nearly-closed path becomes fillable without hand-drawing the last edge. absent
Project / reference Pulls existing 3D edges onto the active workplane as sketch curves. The usual way a second sketch is aligned to the first. absent
Convert to construction Flags a curve as reference-only: it constrains and snaps, but never forms a profile and is never extruded. the flag exists on placements
Scale / rotate selection About a picked origin, with a numeric entry. The transform session already does move; these are the same session with a different manner. session supports all three
Explode Breaks a rectangle, polygon or slot back into its individual edges, so one edge can be edited without the whole shape. absent
Fill Turns a closed loop's transparent face on and off, and decides which side of a nested pair is material. Later this is what separates a hollow extrude from a solid one. built & proven
Answered, and built. Fill is a wish, recorded per loop, and it overrides geometry that would otherwise fill itself. It cannot make an open loop fillable — toggling one on records the wish and takes effect the moment the loop closes. The nesting half is below.

Fill a circle inside a circle is a tube

Two rings. Both are closed, both are planar, so judged on their own merits both fill — and that is exactly the bug: the inner disc gets painted straight over the hole it is supposed to be. Depth settles it instead. Even is material, odd is a hole.

A third ring inside the hole is depth 2 — even — so it fills again, as an island in the middle of the tube. That falls out of the same rule rather than needing another one. Rings on different planes never nest, however neatly they line up when flattened: a circle on the floor and a circle on a wall are two faces, not a tube.

Why the hole is cut rather than drawn over. The fill is transparent. A hole painted in the background colour would be a visible disc rather than a gap, and would still be there the moment the background changed. So the hole is stitched into the outline before the triangles are made: walk the outer ring to the nearest point, cross into the inner ring, walk it the other way round, and cross back. The result is one outline whose interior is the material between the rings.
